What is diabetic Retinopathy?

Diabetic retinopathy is a serious, sight-threatening complication of diabetes and is one of the important causes of vision loss in adults. It occurs when prolonged or poorly controlled high blood sugar levels damage the small blood vessels of the retina, affecting their ability to supply blood and oxygen to the retinal tissues. The condition can particularly affect the macula, the central part of the retina responsible for sharp and detailed vision. As diabetic retinopathy progresses, damaged blood vessels may leak fluid or blood, leading to retinal swelling and other complications that can affect vision. Early detection, regular eye examinations, and appropriate management can help reduce the risk of vision loss.

Understanding the Stages of Diabetic Retinopathy

Diabetic retinopathy can progress through different stages depending on the extent of retinal blood vessel damage.

Mild Non-Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy

This early stage may involve small areas of swelling or leakage from damaged retinal blood vessels. Patients may not notice any changes in vision.

Moderate Non-Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy

As the condition progresses, more retinal blood vessels may become affected, potentially reducing the blood supply to parts of the retina.

Severe Non-Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy

At this stage, a greater number of retinal blood vessels may become blocked, increasing the risk of progression to proliferative disease.

Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy

The retina may respond to reduced blood supply by developing abnormal new blood vessels. These vessels are fragile and may bleed into the vitreous.

High-Risk Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy

Advanced abnormal blood vessel growth can increase the risk of vitreous haemorrhage, retinal traction and significant vision loss.

Advanced Diabetic Eye Disease

Severe diabetic retinal damage may lead to complications such as persistent vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment or significant macular involvement.

What Can Increase the Risk?

Several factors can influence the development and progression of diabetic retinopathy, making regular monitoring important for people with diabetes.

Poor Blood Sugar Control

Persistently high blood sugar levels can contribute to damage to the retinal blood vessels and increase the risk of diabetic retinopathy.

High Blood Pressure

Uncontrolled hypertension can further affect retinal blood vessels and may contribute to progression of diabetic eye disease.

Smoking

Smoking can affect blood vessel health and may increase the risk of complications associated with diabetes.

Who Needs Regular Eye Screening?

People living with diabetes should undergo regular retinal examinations, with the frequency of follow-ups determined by their diabetes status and the presence and severity of retinal changes.

✓
Duration of Diabetes

The likelihood of developing diabetic retinopathy generally increases with the duration of diabetes. Regular eye examinations can help identify changes at an earlier stage.

✓
Type 1 Diabetes

People with type 1 diabetes have a significant risk of developing retinopathy over time and require appropriate retinal screening as advised by their ophthalmologist.

✓
Type 2 Diabetes

Because type 2 diabetes may be present for some time before diagnosis, retinal evaluation is important soon after diagnosis and at intervals recommended by the treating doctor.

✓
Existing Diabetic Retinopathy

Patients who already have retinal changes may require more frequent follow-ups depending on the stage and presence of complications such as macular oedema.

How Is Diabetic Retinopathy Diagnosed?

A detailed retinal evaluation helps identify blood vessel changes, macular involvement, and other complications associated with diabetic eye disease.

Diagnostic Test Purpose
Fundus Examination Allows detailed evaluation of the retina and identification of bleeding, swelling, and other retinal changes.
OCT Provides detailed cross-sectional images of the retina and helps detect macular oedema and other structural changes.
FFA Helps evaluate retinal blood vessels, leakage and areas of reduced blood flow.

Managing Diabetic Retinopathy

Treatment is selected according to the stage of diabetic retinopathy, macular involvement, and the presence of complications.

Blood Sugar Management

Maintaining blood sugar levels within the recommended range is an important part of reducing the risk of diabetic complications and slowing retinal disease progression.

Blood Pressure Management

Good blood pressure control can help protect retinal blood vessels and support overall diabetic eye management.

Laser Photocoagulation

Laser treatment may be recommended for selected patients to manage abnormal retinal blood vessels or areas of retinal damage.

Intravitreal Injections

Anti-VEGF medicines or steroids may be injected into the eye in selected cases, particularly when diabetic macular oedema is affecting vision.

Vitrectomy

Advanced cases involving complications such as significant vitreous haemorrhage or tractional retinal changes may require vitrectomy surgery.

Regular Retinal Monitoring

Continued follow-up helps assess the response to treatment and identify progression or new complications at an early stage.
